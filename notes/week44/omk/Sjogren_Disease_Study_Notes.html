PLO 1LO 1

What is Sjogren's Disease?

PLO 1 — Define Sjogren's Disease and its hallmark features

  • Chronic, inflammatory autoimmune disease of the exocrine glands
  • Hallmark = diminished lacrimal AND salivary gland function
  • Dry eyes (keratoconjunctivitis sicca) + Dry mouth (xerostomia)
  • Clinical triad: Dry eyes + Dry mouth + Arthritis
  • Most serious complication: Non-Hodgkin's lymphoma within 10–15 years

Sjogren's Disease (SjD) is a chronic, inflammatory autoimmune disease primarily targeting the exocrine glands. The hallmark is diminished lacrimal (tear) and salivary gland function — leading to the classic presentations of dry eyes and dry mouth.

⭐ Clinical Triad (High-Yield)

Dry Eyes (Keratoconjunctivitis Sicca) + Dry Mouth (Xerostomia) + Arthritis

Most serious complication: Non-Hodgkin's Lymphoma (within 10–15 years of diagnosis)

Slide 4 — Anatomical targets: lacrimal gland (dry eyes) and salivary glands (dry mouth). Note parotid enlargement.
🔬 From the Lecture

Saliva is critical for protecting teeth, swallowing, beginning digestion, and fighting pathogens (up to a quart/day!). Without it, patients face serious oral consequences — not just discomfort. Loss of tears leaves the cornea vulnerable to scarring and infection.

Beyond glands, systemic (extraglandular) manifestations occur:

  • Extraglandular: arthritis (most common), nephritis, pneumonitis, vasculitis
  • Neurologic: peripheral neuropathy, myelopathy, cognitive issues
💡 Hypersensitivity Type Breakdown

SjD is primarily Type IV (T cell–mediated destruction of epithelial cells). However:

  • Type II — autoantibodies target specific cell/gland receptors
  • Type III — immune complexes released from dead cells deposit in distant tissues → vasculitis, glomerulonephritis, neuropathy
🧠 Exam Question

What is the most serious long-term complication of Sjogren's Disease?

Non-Hodgkin's Lymphoma (MALToma), typically developing within 10–15 years. Driven by chronic B cell stimulation and HIGH BAFF levels. Watch for unilateral parotid swelling + B symptoms (night sweats, weight loss).

PLO 2

Types of Sjogren's Disease

PLO 2 — Distinguish Primary vs Secondary SjD

  • Primary (pSjD): exocrinopathy occurs ALONE
  • Secondary (sSjD): secondary to RA, SLE, or systemic sclerosis
  • Treatment of sSjD targets the underlying disease first

Primary SjD (pSjD)

Exocrinopathy occurs alone — not associated with another autoimmune disease. The glandular dysfunction is the primary pathology.

Secondary SjD (sSjD)

Occurs secondary to another autoimmune disease. Most common underlying conditions:

  • Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)
  • Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)
  • Systemic Sclerosis (Scleroderma)
🏥 Clinical Pearl

If sSjD: treat the underlying autoimmune disease first. Controlling the primary condition often improves Sjogren's symptoms.

Symptoms

PLO 3 — Describe the full spectrum of SjD symptoms

  • Glandular: xerophthalmia, xerostomia, xerorachea, dry skin, vaginal dryness
  • Non-specific: fatigue, arthralgia, ARTHRITIS, myalgia, Raynaud's
  • Extraglandular periepithelial: lung disease, nephritis, GI involvement, myositis, CNS events
  • Extraglandular immunocomplex-mediated: neuropathy, glomerulonephritis, vasculitis
  • Serious: MALToma, cardiovascular complications

⚠️ Important Clinical Note

Dry mouth consequences: Saliva provides mechanical flushing + antimicrobial enzymes. Without it, patients develop oral candidiasis and accelerated dental caries — require dental visits more frequently than every 6 months.

Dry eye consequences: Loss of tear film → corneal damage, scarring, ulceration, infection, potential permanent vision loss.

PLO 4

Disease Characteristics

PLO 4 — Describe the epidemiological and immunological characteristics of SjD

  • Multi-step process: environmental trigger (viral) + genetic susceptibility
  • Strongest genetic link: MHC alleles (better antigen presentation)
  • XX predominance 15–20:1 → estrogen is PROTECTIVE
  • Onset >50 years → coincides with menopause (estrogen drop)
  • Auto-reactive B cells → autoantibody production
Environmental Trigger

Most likely viral infection triggers Type I IFN (IFN-α, IFN-β) response in a susceptible host. This initiates the pathogenic cascade in glandular epithelial cells.

Genetic Susceptibility

Most robust association: MHC gene alleles. Certain alleles allow better presentation of Ro/La autoantigens to CD4+ T cells → break in tolerance.

Sex Hormones

15–20:1 XX predominance. Estrogen is protective — it suppresses autoreactive cells. At menopause (≥50 yrs), estrogen drops → disease emerges. Symptoms onset typically post-50.

B Cell Dysfunction

Auto-reactive B cells are stimulated → produce autoantibodies. Chronic stimulation + high BAFF → risk of malignant transformation (MALToma).

🧠 Exam Question

Why does SjD onset commonly occur after age 50 in XX individuals?

Estrogen is immunoprotective — it suppresses autoreactive lymphocytes. At menopause, estrogen drops precipitously, unmasking previously suppressed autoreactive cells and allowing the disease to manifest.

PLO 5LO 5

Autoantibodies

PLO 5 — Identify the key autoantibodies in SjD and their significance

  • Anti-Ro/SSA (60–80%) — MOST SPECIFIC; Ro52 (E3 ubiquitin ligase) + Ro60 (clears defective RNA)
  • Anti-La/SSB (less common) — NEVER seen without Anti-Ro/SSA
  • ANA — 90% positive; screening tool, not specific to SjD
  • Rheumatoid Factor — 75% affected; not specific to SjD
  • Anti-mAChR — blocks muscarinic receptors → prevents salivary secretion → dry mouth
  • Anti-SSA in pregnancy → fetal complications (congenital heart block)

⭐ High-Yield Antibody Rules
  • Diagnostic order: Screen with ANA first → if positive, test specifically for anti-SSA/SSB
  • SSA without SSB: Possible and common
  • SSB without SSA: Almost NEVER occurs — if you see isolated SSB, reconsider the diagnosis
  • Anti-SSA in pregnancy = urgent — crosses placenta → fetal heart block
🔬 Why Anti-mAChR Causes Dry Mouth

Acetylcholine normally binds muscarinic receptors on salivary gland acini → triggers secretion. Anti-mAChR autoantibodies act as competitive antagonists, blocking this signal even if some gland tissue remains intact. This is why muscarinic agonists (e.g., pilocarpine) can still work therapeutically.

🧠 Exam Question

Can a patient with SjD be anti-SSB positive but anti-SSA negative?

Almost never. Anti-SSB is virtually always accompanied by anti-SSA. Isolated anti-SSB without SSA should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis.

PLO 6LO 6

Pathogenesis

PLO 6 — Explain the pathogenic mechanism of SjD step-by-step

  • Trigger: Viral infection → Type I IFN → epithelial cell (EC) activation
  • ECs upregulate MHC II + B7 costimulation → activate CD4+ T cells directly
  • ECs release IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α, BAFF, and chemokines
  • Chemokines recruit Th1/Th17, dendritic cells → amplify inflammation
  • EC destruction releases Ro/La autoantigens → auto-reactive B cell formation
  • Periductal lymphoid aggregates = tertiary lymphoid structures
  • Chronic B cell stimulation + HIGH BAFF → MALToma
💡 Key Concept: Epithelial Cells as Initiators

In SjD, salivary gland epithelial cells (SGECs) are not just innocent bystanders — they actively initiate and maintain the inflammatory response. This is called an "epitheliitis" phenotype.

  1. Trigger Phase: Environmental factor (likely virus) activates Type I IFN (IFN-α/β) response in genetically susceptible individual. Genetics + sex hormone loss (menopause) predispose.
  2. EC Activation: SGECs are activated by Type I IFN. They upregulate MHC class II and co-stimulatory molecules (e.g., B7/CD80/CD86) — molecules they don't normally express.
  3. T Cell Activation: Activated SGECs directly activate CD4+ T cells via MHC II + B7 → Th1 and Th17 differentiation.
  4. Pro-inflammatory Cytokine Release: SGECs release IL-1, IL-6, TNF-α (classic early inflammatory mediators) and BAFF (B Cell Activating Factor).
  5. Chemokine-Driven Recruitment: Chemokines recruit Th1/Th17 cells and dendritic cells. DCs process and present autoantigens → further amplification.
  6. EC Destruction & Antigen Release: Dying/stressed ECs release intracellular Ro (SSA) and La (SSB) proteins. Stressed cells may even aberrantly express these antigens on their surface.
  7. Autoantibody Formation: Ro/La antigens activate auto-reactive B cells, which cluster in periductal lymphoid aggregates — essentially tertiary lymphoid structures (like an ectopic lymph node).
  8. Malignant Transformation: Chronic B cell stimulation + HIGH BAFF levels → risk of malignant transformation → MALToma (non-Hodgkin's lymphoma).
Slide 10 — Full pathogenesis: trigger phase → SGEC deregulation → chronic inflammation with autoantibody production
⚠️ BAFF is the Key Driver of Lymphoma

High Type I IFN → High BAFF → More B cell activation → Malignant transformation. The BAFF level is a marker for lymphoma risk. This is why therapies targeting BAFF (e.g., belimumab) are being explored.

🧠 Exam Question

Why do salivary gland epithelial cells initiate the immune response in SjD — what is unusual about their MHC expression?

Normally, epithelial cells do NOT express MHC class II or B7 costimulatory molecules. In SjD, Type I IFN activation causes aberrant upregulation of both — turning SGECs into de facto antigen-presenting cells that directly activate CD4+ T cells without requiring professional APCs.

PLO 7

Complications

PLO 7 — List and explain the major complications of SjD

  • Risk of other autoimmune diseases (SLE, RA) — via epitope spreading
  • Corneal scarring, ulcer, rupture, infection → potential blindness
  • MALToma — due to chronic B cell stimulation + HIGH BAFF
  • Renal tubular acidosis type 1 → hypokalemia, kidney stones, acidosis
  • Pregnancy: anti-Ro crosses placenta → congenital heart block (2nd/3rd degree AV block), neonatal lupus, fetal loss
Slide 11 — Complications including the anti-Ro mechanism causing congenital heart block

1. Other Autoimmune Diseases (SLE, RA)

Once tolerance is broken in one autoimmune condition, patients are susceptible to others via epitope spreading. Cell death releases many self-antigens → new autoreactive clones emerge.

2. Corneal Complications

Without adequate tear film: corneal scarring, ulcers, rupture, opportunistic infection → potential permanent vision loss. This is why treatment of dry eyes is medically essential, not just cosmetic.

3. MALToma (B Cell Lymphoma)

⚠️ Clinical Red Flags for Lymphoma

If a SjD patient presents with: unilateral parotid gland enlargement + B symptoms (night sweats, unexplained weight loss, fevers) → immediately suspect MALToma. Biopsy is essential.

4. Renal Tubular Acidosis Type 1 (Distal RTA)

The same autoimmune attack that targets salivary/lacrimal glands also attacks renal tubular epithelial cells. Specifically, cells responsible for H⁺ secretion in the distal tubule are damaged:

  • Cannot excrete H⁺ → metabolic acidosis
  • High urine pH → to maintain electroneutrality, K⁺ is dumped → hypokalemia
  • High urine pH → calcium precipitates → nephrolithiasis (kidney stones)

5. Pregnancy Complications

⚠️ Anti-Ro in Pregnancy — Critical!

Screen all pregnant SjD patients for anti-Ro/SSA before 16 weeks. If positive → consider therapy to suppress antibody production before fetal exposure.

Congenital Heart Block

Anti-Ro IgG crosses placenta → binds cross-reactive antigen on fetal cardiac cells → calcium dysregulation → apoptosis → fibrosis/calcification → 2nd/3rd degree AV block (permanent)

Neonatal Lupus

Maternal anti-Ro/La antibodies → neonatal lupus in baby. Resolves within 6 months after maternal Ab cleared. Advise avoid breastfeeding (Ab in breast milk would prolong symptoms).

Fetal Loss

Autoantibody-mediated damage can lead to miscarriage or fetal death. Requires careful monitoring and possible immunosuppression during pregnancy.

🧠 Exam Question

How does anti-Ro/SSA cause congenital heart block? Walk through the mechanism.

1) Maternal anti-Ro52 IgG crosses placenta → binds cross-reactive antigen on fetal cardiac cells (molecular mimicry).
2) Calcium dysregulation → if caught early, potentially reversible (1st degree AV block).
3) If untreated → apoptosis of cardiac cells → anti-Ro/La antibodies now bind to the apoptotic debris → opsonization → macrophage engulfment → inflammatory reaction in fetal heart → fibrosis + calcification → permanent 2nd/3rd degree AV block.

💡 HIV vs SjD — Biopsy Key

Both can cause gland swelling and sicca. The biopsy cell type is the differentiator: SjD = CD4+ T cells + B cells dominant. HIV = CD8+ T cells dominant (CD4s are depleted by HIV).

💡 Why Checkpoint Inhibitors Cause Sicca

Anti-CTLA-4 and anti-PD-1 therapies remove brake signals from T cells, allowing autoreactive T cells to attack self-tissues including exocrine glands — essentially inducing iatrogenic SjD-like sicca syndrome.

PLO 9LO 9

Diagnosis

PLO 9 — Describe the diagnostic workup for SjD

  • No single definitive test — requires combination of findings
  • Step 1: Typical clinical features (ocular + oral dryness) + rule out other causes
  • Step 2: Serology — ANA (screen), anti-SSA* (most specific), anti-SSB
  • Step 3: Objective dryness — Schirmer's test (<5mm/5min) + slit lamp + whole sialometry
  • Step 4: Biopsy (if anti-SSA negative) — look for foci of ≥50 lymphocytes in salivary glands
Slide 13 — Diagnostic tests: Schirmer's, slit lamp stains, sialometry, biopsy

Step 1 — Clinical History & Exclusion

Ask descriptive questions: "Is your eye gritty? Sandy? Burning?" Ocular + oral dryness history. Exclude: recent transplant, cancer history, checkpoint inhibitor use, viral infections, medications (antihistamines, antipsychotics).

Step 2 — Serology

ANA (Screening)

First-line screening. ~90% SjD positive. NOT specific — also positive in SLE, RA, and other conditions. A positive ANA says "there's likely some autoimmunity here."

Anti-SSA / Anti-SSB (Specific)

Anti-SSA most specific for SjD. Combined with objective findings → strong diagnostic confirmation. Anti-SSA critical to check in pregnancy.

Step 3 — Objective Eye Tests

Schirmer's Test

Filter paper placed under lower eyelid. Measure moisture after 5 minutes. <5mm = decreased tear production. Simple, objective, quantitative.

Rose Bengal Stain

Binds damaged corneal/conjunctival epithelium. Highly effective but painful for patient → largely replaced by fluorescein + lissamine green.

Fluorescein Stain

Pooling in areas of complete cell loss. Also measures tear break-up time — if the green film breaks apart, the tear film is disrupted. Non-vital stain (pools in missing tissue).

Lissamine Green

Like Rose Bengal — binds damaged conjunctival cells. Less painful. Used for conjunctival assessment after fluorescein.

Step 4 — Objective Oral Test

🏥 Whole Sialometry

Patient does NOT eat/chew gum beforehand (no stimulation). Over 10 minutes, collects unstimulated saliva into a cup. Cup is weighed. Below a threshold = objective oral dryness confirmed.

Step 5 — Biopsy (if needed)

🏥 Minor Salivary Gland Biopsy

Used when anti-SSA is low/absent but clinical picture suggests SjD. Typically labial (lip) salivary glands. Look for: gland destruction + dense lymphocytic infiltration with foci ≥50 lymphocytes. Imaging (ultrasound/MRI) also used.

⭐ Diagnostic Confidence Summary

High confidence: Anti-SSA positive + objective dry eye and dry mouth + lymphocytic infiltrate on biopsy.

Challenging cases: Anti-SSA negative/borderline patients with objective dryness — biopsy becomes essential.

No single test is pathognomonic. Diagnosis is cumulative, scored by a point system.

🧠 Exam Question

What is the Schirmer's test and what value indicates pathology?

Filter paper placed under the lower eyelid for 5 minutes. Less than 5mm of wetting = decreased tear production = positive finding for sicca.

🧠 Exam Question

Why was Rose Bengal stain replaced in clinical practice?

It is highly effective at identifying damaged corneal/conjunctival epithelium, but it is very painful for the patient. It has been replaced by the sequential use of fluorescein (for tear break-up time and corneal damage) followed by lissamine green (for conjunctival damage).

PLO 10

Treatment

PLO 10 — Describe management strategies for SjD

  • Secondary SjD: treat underlying autoimmune disease first
  • Dry mouth: dental care, hydration, artificial saliva, muscarinic agonists (pilocarpine)
  • Dry eyes: artificial tears, topical cyclosporine (immunosuppression), lubricants
  • Extraglandular: oral/IV corticosteroids, hydroxychloroquine (↓ antigen presentation)
If Secondary SjD

Treat the underlying autoimmune disease (RA, SLE, systemic sclerosis) — this is the primary intervention.

Dry Mouth

Regular dental care (more frequent than every 6 months). Hydration. Artificial saliva. Muscarinic agonists (e.g., pilocarpine) stimulate remaining functional gland tissue.

Dry Eyes

Artificial tears. Topical cyclosporine (immunosuppression of local inflammatory response). Lubricant eye drops/ointments.

Extraglandular Disease

Corticosteroids (oral/parenteral) — broad immunosuppression for acute flares. Hydroxychloroquine — inhibits antigen processing and presentation in lysosomes.

💡 Mechanism: Why Muscarinic Agonists Work

Even with significant gland destruction, some functional acinar cells remain. Anti-mAChR antibodies are also blocking ACh-mediated secretion. Muscarinic agonists (e.g., pilocarpine, cevimeline) bypass the blocked receptors or outcompete the autoantibodies → stimulate residual secretion.

💡 Mechanism: Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ)

HCQ accumulates in lysosomes and raises their pH → impairs lysosomal antigen processing → less peptide loaded onto MHC → decreased antigen presentation to T cells → reduced autoimmune activation. Used widely in lupus and SjD.

🧠 Exam Question

How does hydroxychloroquine treat autoimmune diseases like SjD?

It raises lysosomal pH, which impairs processing of antigens within APCs. Less peptide is loaded onto MHC class II molecules, reducing antigen presentation to CD4+ T cells and dampening the autoimmune response.

Summary

⭐ The Big 10 — Sjogren's Disease
  1. Definition: Chronic inflammatory autoimmune disease of exocrine glands
  2. Types: Primary (alone) vs Secondary (underlying RA/SLE/sclerosis)
  3. Triad: Dry eyes + Dry mouth + Arthritis; also neurologic + extraglandular
  4. Characteristics: Environmental (viral) trigger + MHC alleles + XX >50 yrs (estrogen ↓)
  5. Autoantibodies: Anti-Ro/SSA (most specific), ±SSB, ANA, RF, anti-mAChR
  6. Pathogenesis: Virus → Type I IFN → SGEC activation → MHC II/B7 upregulation → CD4+ infiltration → Ro/La release → autoreactive B cells → MALToma
  7. Complications: Other AID (epitope spreading), corneal damage, MALToma, RTA type 1, pregnancy (heart block, neonatal lupus)
  8. DDx: Age/drug sicca, IgG4-RD, benign sialadenitis, HCV, HIV, sarcoidosis, GVHD, checkpoint inhibitors
  9. Diagnosis: Clinical sicca + anti-SSA + Schirmer's + slit lamp + sialometry ± biopsy
  10. Treatment: Underlying disease (sSjD) → dry mouth/eye therapy → corticosteroids/HCQ for systemic disease
